How the numbers were derived

  • Only additive balance components are used. Eurostat also publishes attributed memo aggregates — BIOE (Bioenergy) and FE (Fossil energy) — which include the fuel embodied in delivered heat and electricity. Using them would double-count: for Swedish households in 2023, BIOE reads 106,436 TJ while the additive renewables component RA000 reads 34,156 TJ, because most Swedish district heat is biomass-fired. The verified additive identity used here is E7000 + H8000 + RA000 + O4000XBIO + G3000 + solids = TOTAL.
  • Bioenergy is therefore taken as the residual of RA000 after removing the renewable carriers drawn as their own bands (hydro, wind, solar, ambient heat, geothermal).
  • CHP fuel inputs are split between the electricity and district-heating boxes in proportion to each fuel’s gross electricity vs. gross heat output (from nrg_bal_peh).
  • Ambient heat (heat pumps): the part entering district heating comes from TI_EHG_E [RA600]; the part captured by building heat pumps is reported by Eurostat as non-specified and is allocated 80% residential / 20% commercial.
  • Industrial waste heat is Eurostat fuel class X9900 feeding district heating (flue-gas condensation and recovered industrial heat); its input is taken equal to its output.
  • Commercial & services combines commercial/public services, agriculture, forestry and fishing.
  • Coal & peat includes manufactured gases (coke-oven and blast-furnace gas) and peat. Non-energy use (feedstocks, lubricants, bitumen) is a separate terminal. Marine bunkers are excluded per Eurostat convention; international aviation is included in transport.
  • End use applies the LLNL efficiency assumptions: residential and commercial 65%, industrial 49%, transport 21%.

Primary-energy convention (important when reading band widths)

Eurostat uses the physical energy content method, which does not treat all generating sources alike. Verified from the balance tables:

Nuclear therefore enters the chart as reactor heat, roughly 2.8× the electricity it yields, while hydro, wind and solar enter as generated electricity. Band widths are not comparable across source types, and the rejected energy leaving the electricity node is largely a consequence of this convention rather than a measure of the relative efficiency of the sources. Japan applies a different convention again (a single 41.8–42.4% factor to all generating sources), so band widths must not be compared between the two charts.

Comparison with the IEA energy Sankey

For 2023 the IEA reports a total energy supply of 1,892 PJ against 1,975 PJ here. The two agree almost exactly where conventions coincide; the differences are accounting conventions, not data:

  • Per fuel, IEA vs. this chart (PJ, 2023): biofuels & waste 573.5 vs. 570.4; hydro 238.3 vs. 238.3; wind + solar + other 177.1 vs. 177.1; oil 373.5 vs. 367.5; natural gas 30.1 vs. 28.2.
  • Nuclear: the IEA imputes a fixed 33% reactor efficiency (529 PJ); Eurostat reports actual Swedish reactor heat, about 36% (485 PJ). Both describe the same 48.5 TWh of electricity.
  • Electricity exports: the IEA headline nets out net exports (−103 PJ); this chart draws them as an explicit flow. The IEA’s own by-source total is 1,983 PJ — within 0.4% of this chart.
  • Coal: the IEA reports supply (62 PJ, including stock change and the steelworks coke/blast-furnace-gas chain); this chart shows use (35 PJ), with the remainder inside “Own use & losses”.

How the numbers were derived

  • Sign convention: in the METI table, transformation rows are negative for fuel consumed and positive for energy produced. Primary supply is row #190000 (国内供給), already net of exports.
  • Aggregated bands net out product manufacture inside the band. Coal covers coal and coal products, oil covers crude and oil products, gas covers natural gas and city gas. Because city gas is manufactured from natural gas (plus about 96 PJ of LPG feedstock from the oil band), the rows for coke ovens, refineries, gas works and inter-product transfers (#210000, #220000, #230000, #280000) are netted into each band — otherwise the natural gas that becomes city gas would be counted twice. Stock change (#350000) is included as supply, since a stock drawdown is energy supplied to the system that year.
  • Electricity generation merges utility power (事業用発電, including pumped-storage losses) and auto-producers (自家用発電, which contains most on-site and rooftop solar PV).
  • Steam & heat supply merges industrial auto-steam boilers (自家用蒸気) with the district heat-supply business (熱供給); most of its output returns to manufacturing.
  • Sectors: Industrial = 農林水産鉱建設業 + 製造業; Commercial & services = 業務他; each is shown net of the non-energy use booked inside it, which is drawn as its own terminal.
  • Waste & recovered energy is 未活用エネルギー (refuse-fired power, waste tyres and plastics, recovered industrial heat).
  • Renewables are split using METI’s $N detail columns: solar PV and solar heat, wind, biomass, geothermal. Nuclear, hydro, geothermal and PV use METI’s primary-equivalent accounting (thermal input for nuclear and geothermal; generated electricity for hydro, PV and wind).
  • Own use & losses is the residual of each band after conversion, final and non-energy use, so it bundles transformation losses, energy-industry own use, transmission losses and the statistical difference — matching how the LLNL charts present these.
  • End use applies the LLNL efficiency assumptions: residential and commercial 65%, industrial 49%, transport 21%.

Primary-energy convention (important when reading band widths)

METI uses the substitution method: every electricity-generating primary source is converted to primary-energy equivalent at a single reference efficiency. Recovered from METI’s own natural-units sheet (generation in GWh × 3.6 ÷ the primary energy booked):

SourceGenerated (FY2023)Primary bookedImplied factor
Nuclear84,055 GWh724,001 TJ41.80%
Hydro75,210 GWh647,815 TJ41.80%
Solar PV96,458 GWh830,836 TJ41.80%
Wind10,489 GWh90,344 TJ41.80%

The factor is 41.80% in FY2023 and 42.41% in FY2024, applied identically to all four sources. Their band widths are therefore mutually comparable, but each is about 2.4× the electricity actually generated. Geothermal is the exception, reported directly as heat. Rejected energy leaving the electricity node consequently includes this accounting artefact rather than only physical losses. Sweden uses a different convention (nuclear as reactor heat at 36%, renewables as electricity at 100%), so band widths must not be compared between the two charts.

Because band widths represent throughput including stock change and intra-band transformation, the bands sum to slightly more than METI’s domestic-supply headline (17,629 vs. 17,558 PJ in FY2023, a 0.4% difference). The headline figure above is METI’s own and is the one to cite.

Comparison with the IEA energy Sankey

For 2023 the IEA reports a total energy supply of 15,844 PJ (calendar year) against METI’s 17,558 PJ (fiscal year). The ≈1,714 PJ gap is fully explained by four convention differences — neither figure is wrong:

  • Calorific basis (≈ +970 PJ): METI books fuels at gross calorific value (HHV), the IEA at net (LHV). This raises gas by about 354 PJ, oil 409 PJ and coal 204 PJ. Japanese national statistics always sit a few percent above IEA figures for this reason.
  • Renewable electricity accounting (≈ +916 PJ): METI converts hydro, solar and wind electricity to primary equivalent by dividing by average fossil-plant efficiency (implied factor 41.8%: IEA hydro 271 PJ of actual electricity vs. 648 PJ here). The IEA counts these 1:1. Partly offset by nuclear (METI actual ≈38.5% heat, 724 PJ, vs. IEA fixed 33%, 917 PJ: −193 PJ) and geothermal (IEA imputes 10% efficiency: −93 PJ).
  • Recovered energy (≈ +273 PJ): METI counts recovered industrial waste heat and electricity (未活用エネルギー) as primary supply; the IEA does not.
  • Fiscal vs. calendar year plus LHV conversion of biomass and waste: the remainder (≈ −150 PJ).

A consequence of the substitution method: roughly 0.9 EJ of the rejected energy leaving the electricity box is an accounting artefact for hydro, solar and wind rather than physical waste heat. LLNL uses the same convention in its U.S. chart.
